The Role

Product Data Science is a Specialty Within Data Science

Data Science
  • Builds models to predict outcomes — churn, LTV, recommendations
  • Analyzes trends and patterns in historical data
  • Asks: "What is happening? What will happen?"
Product Data Science
  • Designs and interprets A/B tests to measure true feature impact
  • Disentangles correlation from causation before shipping decisions
  • Asks: "If we change X, what actually happens to the outcome?"
The Problem

Prediction is not causation.

A model can say "this feature is important." It cannot say "changing this feature will improve outcomes."
models
= good at finding patterns
product decisions
= need to move outcomes
product data science
= the discipline that connects them
In practice
A general DS finds that users who complete onboarding churn 40% less. A product DS asks: does finishing onboarding cause retention — or do users who plan to stay just happen to bother finishing it?
A general DS finds users of the premium dashboard have 3× higher LTV. A product DS asks: does using the dashboard create that value — or do high-value customers simply explore more features by nature?

CASE STUDY 1

When product teams trust model output too literally

You're on the ranking team at a content platform. Your ML model predicts engagement (likes, comments, shares) on posts in a feed.
Feature importance analysis comes back. The #1 driver is "time since post creation" with an importance score of 0.23. The team must decide: does this mean "boost recent posts"?
FEATURE IMPORTANCE
💬 Your product manager says: "Boost recent posts. That's what people want."
CASE STUDY 1 YOUR DECISION

What do you recommend?

A
Ship the recency boost
Give posts under 2 hours a 2x multiplier. The model says recency matters most — let's optimize for it.
Common Choice
The Trap
  • The model didn't learn that users prefer recent content — it learned the platform's own behavior
  • The feed was already chronological, so recent posts got more impressions, which produced more engagement
  • That's a feedback loop — the model copied the feed design, not a real user preference
  • Boosting recency further amplifies that loop. Nothing about what users actually want has changed
💡 Feature importance tells you what the model learned from your data — not what users actually prefer.
B
A/B test the recency boost first
Run a controlled experiment: treatment gets the 2x recency multiplier, control gets baseline ranking.
Better
Better, But Incomplete
  • You run the test and see a small lift — but it's likely from novelty, not recency
  • Boosting recent posts replaces content users may have already seen with posts they haven't encountered yet
  • Users engage slightly more with unseen content — not because it's recent, but because it's new to them in that session
  • If you'd boosted any unseen posts to the top, you'd probably see the same result
  • The test can't separate "users prefer recent content" from "users engage with anything they haven't seen before"
💡 The bump tells you the metric moved. It doesn't tell you why — and you need to know why before you can scale it.
C
Challenge the causal assumption
Ask: "Does recency cause engagement, or does exposure?" Design a test that separates quality + relevance from recency.
Best Practice
The Causal Reframe
  • You pit two ranking strategies head to head: recency-only vs. quality + relevance
  • Quality + relevance ranks by fit: how often has this user engaged with this creator? How high is this post's engagement rate per impression? Age doesn't factor in
  • A 3-day-old post from a creator you always engage with beats a post from 10 minutes ago from an account you followed once and never touched
  • Quality wins — users engage more and return more often
  • Recency was a proxy for "posts users haven't seen yet" — not a cause of engagement in itself
💡 The real lever wasn't time — it was relevance to that specific user. That's a completely different product decision.

CASE STUDY 1 · REVEAL

The Question That Changes Everything

User scrolls feed (chronological bias)
→ Sees recent posts first
→ More impressions on recent posts
→ More engagement (likes, comments)
  • The PM asked: "What does the model say is important?" — the answer was recency
  • The right question was: "If we change recency in the product, will users actually behave differently?" — the answer was no
  • When we tested relevance ranking instead, users engaged more and returned more often
  • The model didn't lie — it accurately described patterns in the data. The team asked the wrong question of it
Feature importance answers "what did the model learn?"
It doesn't answer "what should we build?" — that's your job.
CASE STUDY 2

The Retention Problem

You're a data scientist on a mobile app team. Retention is falling and leadership wants to act. The analytics team runs a cohort analysis comparing 30-day retention across users who did and didn't enable push notifications.
The numbers come back. The gap is large and clean. The team must decide: is notification opt-in a causal lever for retention, or just a pattern in the data?

💬 Your product manager says: "Get more people to enable notifications! If we move 50% of new users, we'll boost retention by 8-9 points."
CASE STUDY 2 YOUR DECISION

What do you recommend?

A
Launch the notification push
Build an aggressive onboarding prompt. Get as many users as possible to enable push notifications.
Common Choice
The Confounding Trap
  • Notification opt-in rate jumps — but 30-day retention barely moves
  • Casual users enabled notifications and still churned at the same rate
  • Power users were already retained — the prompt didn't change their behavior
  • You forced the symptom without treating the underlying problem
💡 Opt-in predicts retention because invested users do both. Promoting one doesn't create the other.
B
A/B test the onboarding prompt
Treatment group sees a notification opt-in prompt. Control doesn't. Measure 30-day retention.
Better
Right Instinct, Wrong Design
  • The test shows a 1-2 point lift — but three things changed at once, and you can't separate them
  • The prompt itself: just seeing a screen asking about notifications may increase awareness, regardless of opt-in
  • Actual notifications: users who opted in now receive them — but that's only a subset of treatment
  • Self-selection: users who choose to opt in are already more engaged — their retention was never driven by notifications
  • The PM wants to scale it. You're not confident enough to say yes — and you can't explain why it worked
💡 Randomizing the prompt doesn't randomize opt-in. The lift could be the feature, the friction, or the type of user who said yes.
C
Stratify and isolate the causal effect
Segment by user engagement level. Test notifications only on high-churn-risk users. Separate the notification effect from the power-user effect.
Best Practice
The Causal Design
  • Within low-engagement users, notifications have almost no effect on retention
  • Within high-engagement users, they're a small convenience — not a meaningful driver
  • The 17-point gap disappears when you control for engagement level
  • The real retention problem is onboarding and content quality — not notifications
💡 When you see a correlation, ask: what confounder could explain this? Stratify to control for it.

Correlation tells you two things move together. It doesn't tell you which one is the handle.
Find the confounder before you build the intervention.
Your Takeaway

The 6-Question Checklist

These are the questions I wish someone had handed me on day one.
1
Correlation
What does the data show is correlated with my outcome?
2
Causation
Why do we think this correlation exists? What's the mechanism?
3
Confounders
What else could explain this correlation?
4
Intervention
If we change this, will the outcome change — or just the exposure?
5
Experiment
What design isolates the causal effect?
6
Scale
If this works, can we scale it? Or is it fragile?
Most data scientists can build models.
Not many can think causally.
That's the difference between
describing the world and changing it.
Your models will keep lying. Learn to ask the right questions, and you'll know when to trust them — and when to dig deeper.
